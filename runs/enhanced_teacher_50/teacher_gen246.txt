Question: Let \$R\$ be a unit square region and \$n \geq 4\$ an integer. A point \$X\$ in the interior of \$R\$ is called ''n-ray'' partitional if there are \$n\$ rays emanating from \$X\$ that divide \$R\$ into \$n\$ triangles of equal area. How many points are \$100\$-ray partitional but not \$60\$-ray partitional?
<math>
\textbf{(A)}\ 1500 \qquad
\textbf{(B)}\ 1560 \qquad
\textbf{(C)}\ 2320 \qquad
\textbf{(D)}\ 2480 \qquad
\textbf{(E)}\ 2500 </math>

Solution chain-of-thought reasoning: There must be four rays emanating from \$X\$ that intersect the four corners of the square region. Depending on the location of \$X\$, the number of rays distributed among these four triangular sectors will vary. We start by finding the corner-most point that is \$100\$-ray partitional (let this point be the bottom-left-most point).
We first draw the four rays that intersect the vertices. At this point, the triangular sectors with bases as the sides of the square that the point is closest to both do not have rays dividing their areas. Therefore, their heights are equivalent since their areas are equal. The remaining \$96\$ rays are divided among the other two triangular sectors, each sector with \$48\$ rays, thus dividing these two sectors into \$49\$ triangles of equal areas.
Let the distance from this corner point to the closest side be \$a\$ and the side of the square be \$s\$. From this, we get the equation \$\frac{a\times s}{2}=\frac{(s-a)\times s}{2}\times\frac1{49}\$. Solve for \$a\$ to get \$a=\frac s{50}\$. Therefore, point \$X\$ is \$\frac1{50}\$ of the side length away from the two sides it is closest to. By moving \$X\$ \$\frac s{50}\$ to the right, we also move one ray from the right sector to the left sector, which determines another \$100\$-ray partitional point. We can continue moving \$X\$ right and up to derive the set of points that are \$100\$-ray partitional.
In the end, we get a square grid of points each \$\frac s{50}\$ apart from one another. Since this grid ranges from a distance of \$\frac s{50}\$ from one side to \$\frac{49s}{50}\$ from the same side, we have a \$49\times49\$ grid, a total of \$2401\$ \$100\$-ray partitional points. To find the overlap from the \$60\$-ray partitional, we must find the distance from the corner-most \$60\$-ray partitional point to the sides closest to it. Since the \$100\$-ray partitional points form a \$49\times49\$ grid, each point \$\frac s{50}\$ apart from each other, we can deduce that the \$60\$-ray partitional points form a \$29\times29\$ grid, each point \$\frac s{30}\$ apart from each other. To find the overlap points, we must find the common divisors of \$30\$ and \$50\$ which are \$1, 2, 5,\$ and \$10\$. Therefore, the overlapping points will form grids with points \$s\$, \$\frac s{2}\$, \$\frac s{5}\$, and \$\frac s{10}\$ away from each other respectively. Since the grid with points \$\frac s{10}\$ away from each other includes the other points, we can disregard the other grids. The total overlapping set of points is a \$9\times9\$ grid, which has \$81\$ points. Subtract \$81\$ from \$2401\$ to get \$2401-81=\boxed{\textbf{(C)}\ 2320}\$.

--------------------------------------------------
Step 0: Define the unit square region \(R\) and let \(n = 100\) for the first part and \(n = 60\) for the second part.

Step 1: Understand that a point \(X\) is \(n\)-ray partitional if \(n\) rays from \(X\) divide the square into \(n\) triangles of equal area.

Step 2: For \(n = 100\), determine the configuration of rays emanating from \(X\) that will divide \(R\) into \(100\) triangles.

Step 3: Identify the corner-most point in the square region that allows for \(100\) rays to intersect the corners of the square region.

Step 4: Set up the relationship between the areas of the triangular sectors created by rays and the requirement that these areas are equal.

Step 5: From the corner point, establish the equation for the area of triangles formed and solve for the distance \(a\) from this point to the closest side of the square.

Step 6: Calculate that \(a = \frac{s}{50}\), confirming that point \(X\) is \(\frac{1}{50}\) of the side length away from the two nearest sides.

Step 7: Move point \(X\) horizontally and vertically to derive other possible \(100\)-ray partitional points, resulting in a grid of points spaced \(\frac{s}{50}\) apart.

Step 8: Count the number of \(100\)-ray partitional points which forms a \(49 \times 49\) grid, leading to a total of \(2401\) points.

Step 9: For \(n = 60\), determine the configuration of rays that will allow for \(60\) triangles of equal area.

Step 10: Calculate the distance from the corner-most \(60\)-ray partitional point to the sides closest to it.

Step 11: Establish the overlap of \(60\)-ray partitional points with that of \(100\)-ray partitional points, noting that they form a \(29 \times 29\) grid with points \(\frac{s}{30}\) apart.

Step 12: Identify common divisors of \(30\) and \(50\) to understand the overlapping grid characteristics.

Step 13: Calculate the total overlapping points resulting in \(9 \times 9 = 81\) overlapping points.

Step 14: Subtract the overlapping points from the total number of \(100\)-ray partitional points: \(2401 - 81 = 2320\).
