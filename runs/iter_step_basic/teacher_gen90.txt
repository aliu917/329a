Question: By a proper  of a  we mean a   divisor other than 1 and the number itself. A natural number greater than 1 will be called ''nice'' if it is equal to the product of its distinct proper divisors. What is the sum of the first ten nice numbers?

Solution chain-of-thought reasoning: Let \$p(n)\$ denote the product of the distinct proper divisors of \$n\$. A number \$n\$ is ''nice'' in one of two instances:
#It has exactly two distinct  divisors.
#:If we let \$n = pq\$, where \$p,q\$ are the prime factors, then its proper divisors are \$p\$ and \$q\$, and \$p(n) = p \cdot q = n\$.
#It is the cube of a prime number.
#:If we let \$n=p^3\$ with \$p\$ prime, then its proper divisors are \$p\$ and \$p^2\$, and \$p(n) = p \cdot p^2 =n\$.
We now show that the above are the only two cases. Suppose that another nice number existed that does not fall into one of these two categories. Then we can either express it in the form \$n = pqr\$ (with \$p,q\$ prime and \$r > 1\$) or \$n = p^e\$ (with \$e \neq 3\$).
In the former case, it suffices to note that \$p(n) \ge (pr) \cdot (qr) = pqr^2 > pqr = n\$.
In the latter case, then \$p(n) = p \cdot p^2 \cdots p^{(e-1)} = p^{(e-1)e/2}\$.
For \$p(n) = n\$, we need \$p^{(e-1)e/2} = p^e \Longrightarrow e^2 - e = 2e \Longrightarrow \$ \$e = 0\$ or \$e = 3\$.
Since \$e \neq 3\$, in the case \$e = 0 \Longrightarrow n = 1\$ does not work.
Thus, listing out the first ten numbers to fit this form, \$2 \cdot 3 = 6,\ 2^3 = 8,\ 2 \cdot 5 = 10,\$ \$\ 2 \cdot 7 = 14,\ 3 \cdot 5 = 15,\ 3 \cdot 7 = 21,\$ \$\ 2 \cdot 11 = 22,\ 2 \cdot 13 = 26,\$ \$\ 3^3 = 27,\ 3 \cdot 11 = 33\$.
ming these yields \$\boxed{182}\$.

Solution steps:
--------------------------------------------------
Step 0: Let \( p(n) \) denote the product of the distinct proper divisors of \( n \).

Step 1: A number \( n \) is ''nice'' if:
- It has exactly two distinct prime divisors.
- If we let \( n = pq \), where \( p, q \) are the prime factors, then its proper divisors are \( p \) and \( q \), and \( p(n) = p \cdot q = n \).

Step 2: A number \( n \) is also nice if it is the cube of a prime number.
- If we let \( n = p^3 \) with \( p \) prime, then its proper divisors are \( p \) and \( p^2 \), and \( p(n) = p \cdot p^2 = n \).

Step 3: Show that these are the only two cases for nice numbers.
- Suppose another nice number exists that does not fall into one of these two categories.
- If \( n = pqr \) (with \( p, q \) prime and \( r > 1 \)), then \( p(n) \ge (pr) \cdot (qr) = pqr^2 > pqr = n \).

Step 4: If \( n = p^e \) (with \( e \neq 3 \)), then \( p(n) = p \cdot p^2 \cdots p^{(e-1)} = p^{(e-1)e/2} \).
- For \( p(n) = n \), we need \( p^{(e-1)e/2} = p^e \).
- This leads to \( e^2 - e = 2e \) or \( e^2 - 3e = 0 \), giving \( e = 0 \) or \( e = 3 \).

Step 5: Since \( e \neq 3 \), if \( e = 0 \), then \( n = 1 \) which does not work.

Step 6: List out the first ten nice numbers:
- \( 2 \cdot 3 = 6 \)
- \( 2^3 = 8 \)
- \( 2 \cdot 5 = 10 \)
- \( 2 \cdot 7 = 14 \)
- \( 3 \cdot 5 = 15 \)
- \( 3 \cdot 7 = 21 \)
- \( 2 \cdot 11 = 22 \)
- \( 2 \cdot 13 = 26 \)
- \( 3^3 = 27 \)
- \( 3 \cdot 11 = 33 \)

Step 7: Summing these yields \( 6 + 8 + 10 + 14 + 15 + 21 + 22 + 26 + 27 + 33 = 182 \).

Final answer: \(\boxed{182}\)
