Let's denote the initial number of candies Tracy had as \( x \).

1. Tracy ate \(\frac{1}{3}\) of the candies, so she ate \(\frac{x}{3}\) candies and was left with \( x - \frac{x}{3} = \frac{2x}{3} \) candies.
2. She then gave \(\frac{1}{4}\) of what remained to her friend Rachel. So she gave \(\frac{1}{4} \times \frac{2x}{3} = \frac{x}{6}\) candies to Rachel, and was left with \(\frac{2x}{3} - \frac{x}{6} = \frac{4x}{6} - \frac{x}{6} = \frac{3x}{6} = \frac{x}{2} \) candies.
3. Tracy and her mom each ate 15 candies. So they ate a total of \( 15 + 15 = 30 \) candies. Therefore, Tracy was left with \(\frac{x}{2} - 30 \) candies.
4. Tracy's brother then took somewhere from one to five candies, leaving Tracy with three candies. Let's denote the number of candies Tracy's brother took as \( b \) where \( 1 \leq b \leq 5 \). So we have \(\frac{x}{2} - 30 - b = 3\). Rearranging gives \(\frac{x}{2} - 30 - 3 = b \) or \(\frac{x}{2} - 33 = b \). Since \( b \) is an integer between 1 and 5, \(\frac{x}{2} - 33 \) must also be an integer between 1 and 5. Therefore, \(\frac{x}{2} \) must be an integer between 34 and 38. The only integer value for \(\frac{x}{2} \) in this range is 35, so \( x = 70 \).

Let's verify:
- Tracy starts with 70 candies.
- She eats \(\frac{70}{3} \approx 23.33 \) (but since we can't break candies, she eats 23 candies, leaving her with 47).
- She gives \(\frac{47}{4} = 11.75 \) (but since we can't break candies, she gives 11 candies, leaving her with 36).
- Tracy and her mom each eat 15 candies, so they eat 30 candies, leaving her with 6.
- Her brother takes 3 candies, leaving her with 3 candies.

Thus, the initial number of candies Tracy had is \(\boxed{70}\).
