To determine the probability that a sequence of ten 0s and/or 1s does not contain two consecutive 1s, we need to count the number of valid sequences and divide by the total number of possible sequences.

First, let's define \(a_n\) as the number of valid sequences of length \(n\) that do not contain two consecutive 1s. We can break this down into two cases:
1. The sequence ends in 0.
2. The sequence ends in 1.

If the sequence ends in 0, the preceding \(n-1\) digits can be any valid sequence of length \(n-1\). If the sequence ends in 1, the digit before the 1 must be 0, and the preceding \(n-2\) digits can be any valid sequence of length \(n-2\).

This gives us the recurrence relation:
\[a_n = a_{n-1} + a_{n-2}\]
with initial conditions \(a_1 = 2\) (the sequences are 0 and 1) and \(a_2 = 3\) (the sequences are 00, 01, and 10).

We can now compute the values of \(a_n\) up to \(a_{10}\):
\[
\begin{aligned}
a_1 &= 2, \\
a_2 &= 3, \\
a_3 &= a_2 + a_1 = 3 + 2 = 5, \\
a_4 &= a_3 + a_2 = 5 + 3 = 8, \\
a_5 &= a_4 + a_3 = 8 + 5 = 13, \\
a_6 &= a_5 + a_4 = 13 + 8 = 21, \\
a_7 &= a_6 + a_5 = 21 + 13 = 34, \\
a_8 &= a_7 + a_6 = 34 + 21 = 55, \\
a_9 &= a_8 + a_7 = 55 + 34 = 89, \\
a_{10} &= a_9 + a_8 = 89 + 55 = 144.
\end{aligned}
\]
So, there are 144 valid sequences of length 10.

The total number of possible sequences of length 10 is \(2^{10} = 1024\).

The probability that a randomly generated sequence of length 10 does not contain two consecutive 1s is:
\[
\frac{144}{1024} = \frac{9}{64}.
\]
Since 9 and 64 are relatively prime, the probability is in its simplest form. Therefore, \(m = 9\) and \(n = 64\), and \(m+n = 9+64 = 73\).
