To solve the problem, we start by finding the length of \(AD\) using the Angle Bisector Theorem. According to the theorem, the angle bisector of \(\angle BAC\) divides the opposite side \(BC\) into segments proportional to the adjacent sides. Thus, we have:
\[
\frac{BD}{DC} = \frac{AB}{AC} = \frac{5}{3}
\]
Let \(BD = 5x\) and \(DC = 3x\). Since \(BD + DC = BC = 7\), we have:
\[
5x + 3x = 7 \implies 8x = 7 \implies x = \frac{7}{8}
\]
Therefore, \(BD = 5x = \frac{35}{8}\) and \(DC = 3x = \frac{21}{8}\).

Next, we use Stewart's Theorem to find \(AD\). Stewart's Theorem states:
\[
AB^2 \cdot DC + AC^2 \cdot BD = AD^2 \cdot BC + BD \cdot DC \cdot BC
\]
Substituting the known values:
\[
5^2 \cdot \frac{21}{8} + 3^2 \cdot \frac{35}{8} = AD^2 \cdot 7 + \frac{35}{8} \cdot \frac{21}{8} \cdot 7
\]
Simplifying each term:
\[
25 \cdot \frac{21}{8} + 9 \cdot \frac{35}{8} = AD^2 \cdot 7 + \frac{35 \cdot 21 \cdot 7}{8^2}
\]
\[
\frac{525}{8} + \frac{315}{8} = AD^2 \cdot 7 + \frac{5390}{64}
\]
\[
\frac{840}{8} = AD^2 \cdot 7 + \frac{5390}{64}
\]
\[
105 = AD^2 \cdot 7 + \frac{5390}{64}
\]
Multiplying through by 64 to clear the fraction:
\[
6720 = 448AD^2 + 5390
\]
\[
330 = 448AD^2
\]
\[
AD^2 = \frac{330}{448} = \frac{165}{224}
\]

Now, we need to find the length \(DE\). Since \(E\) is the second intersection of the angle bisector with the circumcircle, \(E\) is the midpoint of the arc \(BC\) not containing \(A\). By the properties of cyclic quadrilaterals and the fact that \(DE\) is the diameter of circle \(\gamma\), we use the Power of a Point theorem at \(D\):
\[
DE \cdot DA = DB \cdot DC
\]
\[
DE \cdot \sqrt{\frac{165}{224}} = \frac{35}{8} \cdot \frac{21}{8}
\]
\[
DE \cdot \sqrt{\frac{165}{224}} = \frac{735}{64}
\]
\[
DE = \frac{735}{64} \cdot \frac{224}{165\sqrt{224}}
\]
Simplifying:
\[
DE = \frac{735 \cdot 224}{64 \cdot 165 \sqrt{224}}
\]
\[
DE = \frac{735 \cdot 7}{165 \sqrt{224}}
\]
\[
DE = \frac{5145}{165 \sqrt{224}}
\]
\[
DE = \frac{35}{\sqrt{224}}
\]

Finally, we use the fact that \(F\) is the intersection of \(\omega\) and \(\gamma\) other than \(E\). By the properties of the radical axis and the power of a point, we find:
\[
AF^2 = AB \cdot AC = 5 \cdot 3 = 15
\]

Thus, \(AF^2 = \frac{15}{1}\), and \(m+n = 15+1 = \boxed{16}\).
