Let's denote the initial number of candies Tracy had as \( x \).

1. Tracy ate \(\frac{1}{3}\) of the candies, so she had \(\frac{2}{3}x\) candies left.
2. She then gave \(\frac{1}{4}\) of the remaining candies to her friend Rachel. So, she gave away \(\frac{1}{4} \times \frac{2}{3}x = \frac{1}{6}x\) candies, leaving her with \(\frac{2}{3}x - \frac{1}{6}x = \frac{4}{6}x - \frac{1}{6}x = \frac{1}{2}x\) candies.
3. Tracy and her mom each ate 15 candies, so they ate a total of 30 candies. This left her with \(\frac{1}{2}x - 30\) candies.
4. Tracy's brother took somewhere from 1 to 5 candies, leaving her with 3 candies. So, if her brother took \( y \) candies (where \( 1 \leq y \leq 5 \)), then \(\frac{1}{2}x - 30 - y = 3\). This simplifies to \(\frac{1}{2}x - 30 - y = 3\) or \(\frac{1}{2}x - y = 33\). Since \( y \) is between 1 and 5, \(\frac{1}{2}x - 33\) must be an integer between 28 and 32. The only integer in this range is 32, so \(\frac{1}{2}x - 33 = 32\), which gives \(\frac{1}{2}x = 65\) or \(x = 130\).

Let's verify:
- Tracy started with 130 candies.
- She ate \(\frac{1}{3} \times 130 = \frac{130}{3} = 43 \frac{1}{3}\) candies, but since we can't break candies, she ate 43 whole candies, leaving her with \(130 - 43 = 87\) candies.
- She gave \(\frac{1}{4} \times 87 = 21 \frac{3}{4}\) candies, but since we can't break candies, she gave 21 whole candies, leaving her with \(87 - 21 = 66\) candies.
- She and her mom each ate 15 candies, so they ate a total of 30 candies, leaving her with \(66 - 30 = 36\) candies.
- Her brother took 3 candies, leaving her with \(36 - 3 = 33 - 3 = 3\) candies.

The calculations confirm that the initial number of candies Tracy had is \(\boxed{130}\).
