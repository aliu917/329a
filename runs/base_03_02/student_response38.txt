To determine the year when the number of wrens is less than 10% of what it was on June 1, 2004, we need to model the population decrease over time. Let's denote the initial number of wrens on June 1, 2004 as \( P \). The population decreases by 40% each year, which means it retains 60% of its previous value each year. Therefore, the population after \( n \) years can be expressed as:

\[ P_n = P \cdot (0.6)^n \]

We want to find the smallest integer \( n \) such that the population is less than 10% of the initial population \( P \). This can be written as:

\[ P \cdot (0.6)^n < 0.1 \cdot P \]

We can cancel \( P \) from both sides of the inequality (assuming \( P \neq 0 \)):

\[ (0.6)^n < 0.1 \]

To solve for \( n \), we take the natural logarithm of both sides:

\[ \ln((0.6)^n) < \ln(0.1) \]

Using the property of logarithms that \(\ln(a^b) = b \ln(a)\), we get:

\[ n \ln(0.6) < \ln(0.1) \]

Since \(\ln(0.6)\) is a negative number, we divide both sides by \(\ln(0.6)\) and reverse the inequality:

\[ n > \frac{\ln(0.1)}{\ln(0.6)} \]

Now we calculate the numerical value:

\[ \ln(0.1) \approx -2.302585 \]
\[ \ln(0.6) \approx -0.510826 \]
\[ n > \frac{-2.302585}{-0.510826} \approx 4.506 \]

Since \( n \) must be an integer, we round up to the next whole number:

\[ n = 5 \]

This means that after 5 years, the population will be less than 10% of the initial population. Since the initial census was taken on June 1, 2004, 5 years later would be June 1, 2009. Therefore, the year when the number of wrens will be less than 10% of what it was on June 1, 2004 is:

\[ \boxed{2009} \]
