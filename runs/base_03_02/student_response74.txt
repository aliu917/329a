To determine the number of non-similar regular \( n \)-pointed stars, we need to understand the construction of such stars. A regular \( n \)-pointed star is formed by connecting every \( k \)-th point in a sequence of \( n \) points arranged in a circle, where \( k \) is an integer such that \( 1 < k < \frac{n}{2} \) and \( \gcd(k, n) = 1 \). This ensures that the star does not intersect itself and that it forms a single closed loop.

For \( n = 1000 \), we need to find all integers \( k \) such that \( 1 < k < 500 \) and \( \gcd(k, 1000) = 1 \). The number of such integers is given by Euler's totient function \( \phi(1000) \) divided by 2, because for each valid \( k \), \( 1000 - k \) is also a valid \( k \).

First, we calculate \( \phi(1000) \). The prime factorization of 1000 is \( 1000 = 2^3 \times 5^3 \). The totient function is given by:
\[
\phi(1000) = 1000 \left(1 - \frac{1}{2}\right) \left(1 - \frac{1}{5}\right) = 1000 \times \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{4}{5} = 400.
\]
Since we are only interested in \( k \) such that \( 1 < k < 500 \), we divide \( \phi(1000) \) by 2:
\[
\frac{\phi(1000)}{2} = \frac{400}{2} = 200.
\]
Thus, there are 200 non-similar regular 1000-pointed stars.

\[
\boxed{200}
