Solution steps:
--------------------------------------------------
Step 0: Define the T-grid as a \(3 \times 3\) matrix with five entries as \(1\)s and four entries as \(0\)s.

Step 1: Calculate the total number of ways to fill the T-grid with five \(1\)s and four \(0\)s, which is given by \(\binom{9}{5} = 126\).

Step 2: Identify what constitutes a "bad" T-grid, which includes cases where more than one row, column, or diagonal has all entries equal to \(1\).

Step 3: Analyze Case 1, where each player wins once (one \(1\) and one \(0\) form a winning line).

Step 4: For Case 1, count configurations where both players take a row:
- Player \(1\) can pick any of the \(3\) rows.
- Player \(0\) then picks from \(2\) remaining rows.
- Player \(0\) can take \(3\) boxes in the remaining row.
- This gives \(3 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 = 18\) cases.

Step 5: Repeat Step 4 for configurations where both players take a column, which similarly gives another \(18\) cases.

Step 6: Combine results from Case 1: \(18 + 18 = 36\) cases where both players win.

Step 7: Analyze Case 2, where player \(1\) wins twice (player \(0\) cannot win twice due to having only four moves).

Step 8: For Case 2, calculate the configurations where player \(1\) picks a row and a column:
- There are \(3\) ways to pick the row and \(3\) ways to pick the column, yielding \(3 \cdot 3 = 9\) cases.

Step 9: Calculate configurations where player \(1\) picks a row/column and a diagonal:
- There are \(6\) ways to pick the row/column and \(2\) ways to pick the diagonal, yielding \(6 \cdot 2 = 12\) cases.

Step 10: Recognize that there is only \(1\) case where player \(1\) wins with both diagonals filled.

Step 11: Combine results from Case 2: \(9 + 12 + 1 = 22\) cases where player \(1\) wins twice.

Step 12: Subtract the total number of bad T-grids from the total configurations: 
\[ 126 - 36 - 22 = 68 \]

Final answer: \(\boxed{68}\)
