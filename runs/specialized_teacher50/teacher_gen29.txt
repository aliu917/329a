Question: Call a three-term strictly increasing arithmetic sequence of integers special if the sum of the squares of the three terms equals the product of the middle term and the square of the common difference. Find the sum of the third terms of all special sequences.

Solution chain-of-thought reasoning: Let the terms be \$a-b\$, \$a\$, and \$a+b\$. Then we want \$(a-b)^2+a^2+(a+b)^2=ab^2\$, or \$3a^2+2b^2=ab^2\$. Rearranging, we get \$b^2=\frac{3a^2}{a-2}\$. Simplifying further, \$b^2=3a+6+\frac{12}{a-2}\$. Looking at this second equation, since the right side must be an integer, \$a-2\$ must equal \$\pm1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12\$. Looking at the first equation, we see \$a>2\$ since \$b^2\$ is positive. This means we must test \$a=3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 14\$. After testing these, we see that only \$a=5\$ and \$a=14\$ work which give \$b=5\$ and \$b=7\$ respectively. Thus the answer is \$10+21=\boxed{031}\$.
Note: If you don't understand the simplification of \$b^2=\frac{3a^2}{a-2}\$, you can actually just use synthetic division and arrive at the same place

Solution steps:
--------------------------------------------------
Step 0: Let the terms of the arithmetic sequence be represented as \( a - b \), \( a \), and \( a + b \), where \( a \) is the middle term and \( b \) is the common difference.

Step 1: Set up the equation for a special sequence, which requires that the sum of the squares equals the product of the middle term and the square of the common difference: 
\[
(a - b)^2 + a^2 + (a + b)^2 = ab^2.
\]

Step 2: Expand the left side of the equation:
\[
(a - b)^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2,
\]
\[
(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2.
\]
Combining these, we have:
\[
a^2 - 2ab + b^2 + a^2 + a^2 + 2ab + b^2 = 3a^2 + 2b^2.
\]

Step 3: Substitute the left side into the equation:
\[
3a^2 + 2b^2 = ab^2.
\]

Step 4: Rearranging gives:
\[
2b^2 = ab^2 - 3a^2.
\]

Step 5: Factor out \( b^2 \):
\[
b^2 = \frac{3a^2}{a - 2}.
\]

Step 6: Simplifying further leads to:
\[
b^2 = 3a + 6 + \frac{12}{a - 2}.
\]

Step 7: Determine that since \( b^2 \) must be a perfect square and non-negative, \( a - 2 \) must divide \( 12 \). The divisor options are \( \pm1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12 \).

Step 8: Since \( b^2 \) must be positive, we restrict \( a - 2 \) to positive values, meaning \( a - 2 \) can equal \( 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12 \).

Step 9: Calculate potential values for \( a \) from \( a - 2 \):
- For \( a - 2 = 1 \) → \( a = 3 \)
- For \( a - 2 = 2 \) → \( a = 4 \)
- For \( a - 2 = 3 \) → \( a = 5 \)
- For \( a - 2 = 4 \) → \( a = 6 \)
- For \( a - 2 = 6 \) → \( a = 8 \)
- For \( a - 2 = 12 \) → \( a = 14 \)

Step 10: Test each \( a \) value to see if \( b^2 \) is a perfect square:
- For \( a = 3 \): \( b^2 = 3 \) (not a perfect square)
- For \( a = 4 \): \( b^2 = 6 \) (not a perfect square)
- For \( a = 5 \): \( b^2 = 5 \) (not a perfect square)
- For \( a = 6 \): \( b^2 = 6 \) (not a perfect square)
- For \( a = 8 \): \( b^2 = 12 \) (not a perfect square)
- For \( a = 14 \): \( b^2 = 18 \) (not a perfect square)

Step 11: Only valid \( a \) values are \( a = 5 \) and \( a = 14\) giving \( b = 5 \) and \( b = 7 \) respectively.

Step 12: Compute the sum of the third terms of all valid sequences: 
- For \( (5 - 5, 5, 5 + 5) = (0, 5, 10) \)
- For \( (14 - 7, 14, 14 + 7) = (7, 14, 21) \)

Step 13: The third terms are \( 10 \) and \( 21 \). Thus, the sum is \( 10 + 21 = 31 \).
