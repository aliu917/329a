Question: Call a three-term strictly increasing arithmetic sequence of integers special if the sum of the squares of the three terms equals the product of the middle term and the square of the common difference. Find the sum of the third terms of all special sequences.

Solution chain-of-thought reasoning: Let the terms be \$a-b\$, \$a\$, and \$a+b\$. Then we want \$(a-b)^2+a^2+(a+b)^2=ab^2\$, or \$3a^2+2b^2=ab^2\$. Rearranging, we get \$b^2=\frac{3a^2}{a-2}\$. Simplifying further, \$b^2=3a+6+\frac{12}{a-2}\$. Looking at this second equation, since the right side must be an integer, \$a-2\$ must equal \$\pm1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12\$. Looking at the first equation, we see \$a>2\$ since \$b^2\$ is positive. This means we must test \$a=3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 14\$. After testing these, we see that only \$a=5\$ and \$a=14\$ work which give \$b=5\$ and \$b=7\$ respectively. Thus the answer is \$10+21=\boxed{031}\$.
Note: If you don't understand the simplification of \$b^2=\frac{3a^2}{a-2}\$, you can actually just use synthetic division and arrive at the same place

Solution steps:
--------------------------------------------------
Step 0: Let the terms of the arithmetic sequence be denoted as \(a-b\), \(a\), and \(a+b\). 

Step 1: Write the equation representing the condition for the special sequence: \((a-b)^2 + a^2 + (a+b)^2 = ab^2\).

Step 2: Expand the left side of the equation: 
\((a-b)^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2\), 
\(a^2 = a^2\), 
\((a+b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2\). 

Step 3: Combine the expanded terms: 
\((a^2 - 2ab + b^2) + a^2 + (a^2 + 2ab + b^2) = 3a^2 + 2b^2\).

Step 4: Set the combined left side equal to the right side: 
\(3a^2 + 2b^2 = ab^2\).

Step 5: Rearrange this equation to express \(b^2\): 
\(b^2 = \frac{3a^2}{a-2}\).

Step 6: Simplify further to find another expression for \(b^2\): 
\(b^2 = 3a + 6 + \frac{12}{a-2}\).

Step 7: Determine the integer constraints for \(b^2\): since the right side must be an integer, \(a-2\) must equal \(\pm1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12\).

Step 8: Since \(b^2\) must be positive, we deduce that \(a > 2\). Thus, we test the values \(a = 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 14\).

Step 9: Check each of these values to see which yield integer values for \(b\):
- For \(a = 3\): \(b^2 = 3(3) + 6 + 12/1 = 15\) (not perfect square)
- For \(a = 4\): \(b^2 = 3(4) + 6 + 12/2 = 18\) (not perfect square)
- For \(a = 5\): \(b^2 = 3(5) + 6 + 12/3 = 27\) (perfect square, \(b = 5\))
- For \(a = 6\): \(b^2 = 3(6) + 6 + 12/4 = 36\) (perfect square, \(b = 6\))
- For \(a = 8\): \(b^2 = 3(8) + 6 + 12/6 = 48\) (not perfect square)
- For \(a = 14\): \(b^2 = 3(14) + 6 + 12/12 = 60\) (not perfect square)

Step 10: The valid pairs are \((a, b) = (5, 5)\) and \((6, 6)\), giving the third terms \(5 + 5 = 10\) and \(6 + 6 = 12\).

Step 11: Calculate the sum of the third terms: \(10 + 21 = 31\).

Final answer: \(\boxed{31}\)
